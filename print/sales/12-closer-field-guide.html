Know how the claim pays. Never pretend to run it.

Homeowners trust the closer who can explain their claim plainly and who stays on the right side of the adjuster line. This is what you need to know, and where the line is.

How an insurance roof pays

TermWhat it means for the homeowner
Replacement cost (RCV)What it costs to replace the roof new. The carrier's scope and price set it, not us.
DepreciationThe carrier's reduction for the roof's age and wear.
Actual cash value (ACV)Replacement cost minus depreciation. Many policies pay this first.
Recoverable depreciationOn a replacement-cost policy, the depreciation is usually released after the work is done and the final invoice is sent.
ACV-only roof policyDepreciation is never paid back, so the homeowner's share is bigger. Increasingly common in Texas. Ask them to check their declarations page.
DeductibleTheirs to pay, always. Texas wind and hail deductibles are often a percentage of the home's insured value (1% or 2%), not a flat $1,000.

The first check is usually the ACV minus the deductible, often made out to the homeowner and their mortgage company together. PowerEdge's first insurance payment is when half your commission is earned.

Say it this way: "Your policy decides what's covered, not me. What I can tell you is what I found on your roof, and I'll put it in writing with photos. If you file, you'll know exactly what to show your adjuster."

The adjuster line (Tex. Ins. Code § 4102.163)

You may

  • Meet the adjuster on the roof, if the homeowner wants you there
  • Show the damage, your photos and your measurements
  • Point out storm damage the adjuster may have missed
  • Explain PowerEdge's own estimate for the work

You never

  • File, adjust or negotiate the claim for the homeowner
  • Argue coverage or tell them what their policy owes
  • Speak for the homeowner, or sign anything for them
  • Advertise or imply that PowerEdge "handles claims"

When the carrier's scope misses work the roof needs, PowerEdge sends the documentation to the carrier for its review. The homeowner stays the one making the claim. If you're unsure whether something crosses the line, stop and call the office.

The inspection: photos that hold up

  1. Front of the house with the address number in frame.
  2. Each slope, wide, so the whole roof is accounted for.
  3. A test square on each slope, marked and counted, with a close-up of the hits.
  4. Soft metals: vents, flashing, gutters, downspouts. Dents here back up the stone size.
  5. Collateral: window screens, siding, fence, the AC unit's fins.
  6. What isn't storm damage: wear, blistering, old repairs, layers. Write it down too.

Add the photos on the job and record what you found the same day, including whether the homeowner was there. Homeowner there, photos in, first PowerEdge inspection at that address: that's a held inspection, and it pays your knocker $50. Answer the homeowner question truthfully every time; it's what the knocker is paid on. Fill out the inspection report every time, including when the verdict is "your roof is fine."

Presenting the agreement

  1. Write it in the Agreement tab: scope lines and the price.
  2. Point to the deductible notice and read it out loud. It builds more trust than anything else you'll say.
  3. Get their email. It's required: it's how they get their copy and their cancellation notice.
  4. Tell them plainly: "You can cancel until midnight of the third business day after you sign, with no penalty. It's on your copy, with the exact date."
  5. They sign on your phone. Their copy is emailed the moment they do.
Financing: bring it up only after the homeowner says the deductible is the problem, never as an opener. They still pay the full deductible, just over time.
Never: promise coverage or a new roof · waive, cover or "work with" a deductible · say you'll handle their claim · call PowerEdge a licensed roofer · pressure anyone to sign · leave a signed homeowner without their emailed copy.
